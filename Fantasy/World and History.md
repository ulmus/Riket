# World and History

The old order has survived, but it no longer commands the world without question. Kings still inherit crowns. Nobles still collect rents. The Church of the Ascendant still judges marriages, buries the dead and decides which pacts are miracles and which are witchcraft. Around them, cities grow, printers multiply forbidden texts and cannon make ancestral walls obsolete.

There is no newly discovered continent beyond the western sea. Exploration turns towards places that were buried, abandoned or deliberately forgotten. Scholars recover scraped manuscripts from monastery walls. Miners break into sealed chambers. Surveyors find roads beneath forests that did not stand there before the Withering. The past is becoming difficult to suppress.

## The Age of Many Altars

Before the Ascendancy, worship belonged to households, villages, guilds and rulers. A town might keep covenants with a river, a dead founder and a servant of the Flame. The next valley could obey different laws and make offerings to different powers.

The Four Great Others were known, but most communities dealt with Lesser Others. These local powers brought rain, guarded walls, witnessed bargains or demanded blood. Some were generous. Others ruled through fear. A useful god in one place could be a predator in another.

Later pagan writers described this period as an age of freedom. Church histories describe it as an age of terror. Both accounts omit what does not serve them. There was no single pagan faith and no lost golden age.

## The Ascendancy

Several centuries ago, the cult of the Ascendant formed alliances with conquering rulers, city magistrates and noble houses. Its binding oaths ended feuds that no mortal court could settle. Consecrated roads offered safer travel. Sanctuary walls kept hostile Others outside.

Conversion was followed by law and force. Temples were broken or rededicated. Lesser Others that submitted to the Ascendant's Court were declared angels and saints. Those that refused became demons. Pagan priests could accept a place within the Church, flee beyond its reach or die with their shrines.

The Church taught that the Ascendant had always ruled above the other gods. Suppressed accounts claim that it rose to its present position during these wars. No surviving source proves whether the Ascendancy was a mortal conquest, a divine victory or both.

The ruling dynasty that led the first campaigns eventually collapsed. Its conquests divided into kingdoms, marches and free cities, but the Church survived them. Its calendar, courts and rites still extend across hostile borders.

## The Long Accord

The centuries after the Ascendancy are remembered as the Long Accord. Church bells marked settled land. Noble oaths passed obligations from one generation to the next. Monasteries kept records, maintained roads and trained administrators. The Crown protected the Church, and the Church confirmed the Crown's right to rule.

The Accord did not bring constant peace. Kings fought over inheritance, nobles oppressed their tenants and the Church punished dissent. It did make the world more predictable. Most people understood which law judged them, which priest buried them and which walls would hold after sunset.

The rival Courts withdrew rather than vanished. Pagan customs survived as seasonal festivals, household prohibitions and saints with suspiciously old names. The Wild remained beyond cleared land. The Flame waited in kilns and workshops. The Deep slept beneath graves, mines and the sea.

## The Withering

The Withering Plague struck two or three generations ago. It returned in several waves. Victims weakened, dried and turned grey before death. In many regions, one person in four died. Some towns lost far more.

Nobody knows what caused it. Physicians describe a natural contagion. Church authorities blame broken covenants and hidden pagan worship. Witches claim that the plague worsened where the Ascendant's old wards failed. Other accounts connect it to a Lesser Other or to an unintentional movement in the dreams of the Deep.

Processions, pilgrimages and crowded sanctuaries spread the sickness. Priests died beside their congregations. In some villages, outlawed healers succeeded where approved rites failed. The Church endured, but its claim to sole protection never recovered.

The plague changed the balance between those who owned land and those who worked it. Labourers demanded wages and freedom of movement. Survivors inherited workshops and farms. Guilds admitted people who would previously have remained servants. Noble families fought over vacant estates and uncertain bloodlines.

Fields, villages and monasteries stood empty. Forests crossed old boundary stones. Bandits, refugees and pagan communities occupied abandoned estates. Mass graves and sealed houses remain common along roads that never fully reopened.

## The Present Age

The world is entering an early Renaissance. Merchant houses lend money to princes. Paid soldiers fight beside feudal levies. Pike formations protect arquebusiers, and artillery reduces sieges from years to weeks. Printing presses allow sermons, accusations and forbidden histories to reach thousands of readers.

Old learning is returning through excavation rather than overseas conquest. Alchemists compare pagan formulae with natural philosophy. Surgeons study bodies despite Church restrictions. Antiquarians discover that accepted saints once bore other names. Every recovered text creates questions about who altered it and why.

The Four Courts are felt more openly:

- The Ascendant remains powerful through law, worship and repeated rites, but its Church is divided.
- The Wild returns to land abandoned during the Withering and to communities rejecting distant rulers.
- The Flame spreads through foundries, firearms, printing and political revolt.
- The Deep stirs in mines, drowned ruins, shared dreams and graves opened too soon.

The age is unstable rather than apocalyptic. Most harvests still grow. Courts still meet. Markets open each morning. The old institutions continue to function, but fewer people believe they will last unchanged.

## The Realm

The central kingdom is commonly called **the Realm**. Its formal name and the origin of that name remain to be established with its geography.

The Realm is a patchwork of royal lands, noble estates, bishoprics, chartered towns and frontier marches. The Crown claims authority over all of them, but its actual power depends on old privileges, negotiated taxes and the willingness of local rulers to obey. A royal decree may be absolute in the capital and merely an opening demand two days away.

The Church of the Ascendant is equally divided. Bishops defend local rights. Monastic orders control land and archives. Reforming priests demand greater discipline and less corruption. Inquisitors argue that tolerance allowed the rival Courts to return. All claim to defend the same faith.

The capital is the seat of the Crown and one of the Church's greatest centres. It is also a river port, a market, a garrison and a place where nobles maintain competing households. Its districts and name are described in [[Geography of the Realm]].

## Surrounding Powers

The Realm is neither isolated nor surrounded by enemies. Its neighbours trade, intermarry and make war according to interest.

### The Vardessian Empire

The Vardessian Empire is the Realm's principal military rival in the east. Its emperor claims the legacy of the dynasty that led the Ascendancy and presents himself as the rightful protector of the faith. Vardessian bishops obey a rival head of the Church.

The Empire maintains disciplined armies of pikes, cavalry and artillery. Fortresses face one another across a disputed frontier. Both sides expect another war, although neither treasury can easily afford it.

### The Kingdom of Cordaigne

Cordaigne is a wealthy southwestern monarchy joined to the Realm by generations of marriage and disputed inheritance. Its rulers can produce a legal claim to several provinces and, under the right reading of an old marriage contract, to the Realm's throne.

Cordaigne prefers marriage, loans, spies and local allies to open conquest. It shelters exiled nobles and at least one claimant whom the Realm would rather forget.

### The Keldic Crown

The Keldic Crown is a loose northern kingdom of towns, valleys and marcher lordships. Its rulers accepted the Ascendant without erasing every older covenant. Public rites to the Wild remain lawful in some provinces.

Church writers describe Keld as barbarous and half-pagan. Its courts, foundries and trading towns make that claim difficult to maintain. Border raids occur, but Keld is as often a trading partner or military ally as an enemy.

### The Serevan League

The Serevan League is a confederation of southern merchant republics. Its banks finance rulers on every side. Its foundries sell cannon to armies that may later use them against one another.

The League is not formally hostile to the Realm. It gains more through debt, exclusive contracts and hired soldiers than through annexation. Several powerful guilds maintain discreet pacts with Lesser Others of the Flame.

## Enemies of the Realm

Enemies of the Crown, enemies of the Church and enemies of ordinary people are not always the same.

The Crown fears Vardessian invasion, Cordaigne's claimants, rebellious nobles and cities that refuse taxation. The Church fears pagan covenants, unlicensed pacts, reformers and evidence that contradicts its history. A village may fear soldiers, hunger or the local saint more than any foreign emperor.

The abandoned marches contain outlaw lords, deserters and communities that no longer recognise royal law. Some have renewed covenants with the Wild. Others merely prefer the dangerous freedom they gained after the Withering.

Independent Lesser Others present a different threat. They are close enough to mortals to seek a town, bloodline or cult of their own. Some take the names of saints. Others offer useful bargains until their worshippers can no longer refuse them.

**The Withered** is a loose name for plague cults, survivors changed by forbidden cures and isolated communities that never returned to ordinary society. They have no common leader or purpose. The name is also used against innocent survivors when land or inheritance is at stake.

## Sources of Conflict

- **Crown against nobility:** The Crown needs taxes and soldiers. Noble houses defend privileges older than the present dynasty.
- **Church against Church:** Rival bishops, reformers and inquisitors disagree about how the Ascendant's order should be restored.
- **Town against countryside:** Guilds and merchants want free labour and open roads. Landowners want tenants bound to their estates.
- **Old wealth against new money:** Noble titles still command respect, but bankers can decide whether armies march.
- **Accepted faith against forbidden covenant:** A cult may be a predatory conspiracy, a persecuted local religion or both.
- **The living against the buried past:** Graves, ruins and censored records hold claims that can overturn property, doctrine and succession.
- **Mortals against their patrons:** Every miracle and spell comes from an Other. The price may fall on someone who never agreed to the pact.

At the frontier, engineers repair fortresses for the next war with Vardess. In the cities, presses publish competing versions of the faith. Beyond abandoned boundary stones, old shrines receive offerings again. Far below them, the Deep remains almost entirely still.
